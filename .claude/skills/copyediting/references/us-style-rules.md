# US style rules for fiction (CMOS 17/18 defaults, Merriam-Webster spelling)

These are defaults. The project's `style-sheet.md` always wins; record every deviation there.

## 1. Numbers

| Case | Fiction default | Example |
|---|---|---|
| whole numbers zero to one hundred | words | twelve boats, ninety-nine |
| round numbers | words | two hundred, three thousand, five million |
| other large numbers | digits | 1,347 votes (narration), but prefer rewording in prose |
| sentence start | words or reword | Nineteen ninety-four was cold. |
| years | digits | 1994, the 1990s, the '90s |
| decades in words | allowed | the nineties |
| times | words with o'clock; digits with a.m./p.m. | four o'clock, 4:15 a.m., half past four |
| dates | month day, year | March 2, 1994 (never 2nd in dates) |
| ages | words | she was thirty-two; a thirty-two-year-old |
| money | words for round amounts | ten dollars, $3.47 |
| percentages | words in narrative | forty percent |
| addresses, phone, chapter numbers | digits | 221 Mill Lane |
| dialogue | words almost always | "Back by nineteen hundred." |

Consistency rule: in one sentence or paragraph, numbers of the same category share a style.

## 2. Hyphenation and compounds

| Pattern | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| compound modifier before noun | hyphenate | a well-known face; a two-day storm |
| same after noun | open | her face was well known |
| -ly adverb + adjective | never hyphenate | a badly lit room |
| age compounds | hyphenate as noun or modifier | a six-year-old; a six-year-old boy; he is six years old |
| fractions | hyphenate as modifiers and nouns | two-thirds of the town |
| prefixes (re, pre, non, co, anti) | closed unless doubled vowel or proper noun | reopen, nonissue, co-op, anti-American |
| self- | hyphenate | self-aware |
| suspended | hyphen and space | two- and three-room houses |

Closed in MW (common slips): lifeboat, lighthouse, makeup (noun), setup (noun), login (noun),
nowadays, toward, afterward, backyard (noun), firsthand, offshore, overboard, underway (adj.).
Open: ice cream (noun), high school (noun), living room. Look up anything else in MW.

## 3. Capitalization

- Titles and ranks: capitalize before a name (Captain Vell, Aunt Ruth, Doctor Hale); lowercase alone
  or with an article (the captain, my aunt). Direct address may capitalize ("Yes, Captain.").
- Family terms used as names: "Mom said" vs "my mom said".
- God, the Bible capitalized; pronouns for God lowercase by default.
- Seasons, directions lowercase (winter, north of the river) unless part of a name (the North Shore).
- Invented terms: glossary decides; consistency audit enforces.
- After a colon: lowercase unless a full quotation, a proper noun, or two or more full sentences follow.

## 4. Punctuation details

- Possessives: Charles's, the Vells' house, Moses's (CMOS 17/18 adds 's to names ending in s).
- Plurals of names: the Vells, the Joneses; never apostrophes for plurals (the 1990s, not 1990's).
- Semicolons: join closely related independent clauses; separate list items that contain commas.
- Colons in narration are rare in fiction; a dash usually reads better (style call, not an error).
- Question marks inside quotes only if the quote is a question.
- Interrobang, double punctuation (?!) only per style sheet.
- Scene break marker: one style per book (`* * *` or `#`).

## 5. Quotation marks and italics

- Double quotes for dialogue and quoted words; single quotes inside double.
- Curly quotes throughout or straight throughout, never mixed (typography finding).
- Apostrophe at the start of a contraction faces away from the word: ’90s, ’em, rock ’n’ roll
  (word processors often insert an opening quote; check).
- Thoughts: roman without quotes is CMOS-acceptable; italics if the style sheet says so. Never quote
  marks for thought.
- Sounds and words as words: italics (*thunk*; the word *never*).
- Foreign words: italicize unless in MW; do not italicize the dialogue of a character speaking their
  own language rendered in English.

## 6. Grammar points that differ in dialogue

In dialogue keep the character's grammar (who for whom, ain't, double negatives, fragments). Flag
only if it looks like an author slip (the character never talks that way elsewhere) and say so.
